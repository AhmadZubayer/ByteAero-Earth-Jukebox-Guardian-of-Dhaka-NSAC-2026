*The game in one sentence: you play a child in Dhaka who finds dangerous heat by listening, because NASA's satellite heat maps have been turned into music, and then you cool the city with trees, ponds, and white roofs before a heat wave arrives.*

## 1. The problem: why we made this game

### Heat in Dhaka

Dhaka is one of the most crowded cities in the world. In April, before the monsoon rains arrive, the city can get dangerously hot. NASA's weather records for Dhaka show the hottest April day reaching about 40 °C in 2000, 42 °C in 2010 and 43 °C in 2024.

Concrete, roads and tin roofs soak up the sun all day and stay hot. Blocks with trees, grass and water stay cooler. Scientists call this the urban heat island: the most built-up parts of a city become islands of heat.

The people who feel it most are the ones who cannot stay indoors: rickshaw pullers, tea sellers, street vendors, and children walking to school.

### NASA can see the heat, but most people only get a picture

NASA satellites measure how hot the ground is, all over the world, every day. The results are beautiful coloured maps. But a map only works if you can see it and read it. That leaves out people who are blind or have low vision, and many people who find charts hard to understand.

### The challenge we answered

NASA's Space Apps challenge "The Earth Information Jukebox" asks teams to turn NASA's Earth pictures into sound, so that more people can experience Earth science. Sound can also show patterns the eye misses, and it can make people feel something about our planet.

### Our idea

What if you could hear where the heat is? We turned NASA's heat maps of Dhaka into music: hot places play high, bright notes. Then we built a game around it, set in our own city, where finding the heat with your ears is the first step to protecting people from it.

## 2. The game: a step-by-step walkthrough

It runs in any web browser, on a laptop or a phone. It is a 3D game: you walk around streets full of rickshaws, tea stalls, shops and trees. A full play-through takes about half an hour: six years of about 3–4 minutes each, plus the story, the tutorial, the reports and the ending.

### The sounds you will hear (the "sound map")

| Instrument | What it tells you |
| --- | --- |
| **Sitar** | Heat. Higher and brighter notes mean hotter ground. In the city, the sitar plays from where the heat is, and gets louder and faster as you get close. |
| **Tabla** (drum) | Rain. More rain means a busier drum. |
| **Flute** | Green plants. A greener city plays the flute more often. |
| **Drone** (steady hum) | The mood of the city. It goes slightly out of tune when the city is less green, or left in the heat. |

The music uses scales from South Asian classical music (ragas). For example, a tense "Marwa-like" scale plays when many blocks are hot, and the rain raga "Megh Malhar" plays in a rainy year.

### Step 1 — The title screen

- **You see:** the game's name, a **Start** button and a **Sound Lab** button.
- **You can:** open *Settings and accessibility* first. Options include captions for every sound, *Relaxed mode* (no timer), *Reduce motion*, and *Text-only mode* (the whole game as text pages for screen readers).
- **Optional — Sound Lab:** turn Heat, Rain and Green up and down and hear how each one changes the music, with the notes flowing in a live visualiser. Then try a short listening quiz called *Quick Ears*.

### Step 2 — A broken message from 2050

- **You see:** Echo, a small listening satellite-drone, has caught a crackling message from a child in a possible future Dhaka: *"…Can anyone hear… what happened to our city…? …Listen to the heat… help us…"*
- **You learn:** the message is broken into **six pieces**. Every year you protect brings one piece back. (This is the story thread that ties the whole game together.)
- **You do:** type your name for the ticket you get at the end, and press **Help Echo**.

### Step 3 — The Earth Jukebox globe

- **You see:** a spinning globe coloured by NASA's real global temperature record. Press **Play the time-lapse** and it runs from **1884 to 2022**: blue (cooler) slowly turns yellow, orange and red (warmer).
- **You hear:** each sitar note is one year of NASA's temperature record. The melody climbs and gets tenser as the world warms.
- **You learn:** by 2022 the world was about **1.1 °C warmer than in the 1880s**. This is a long, 140-year record, which is how scientists see climate change.
- **You do:** press **Fly to Dhaka**. The camera dives down to Bangladesh.

### Step 4 — The tutorial (about one minute)

A practice street teaches one thing at a time:

1. **Walk** to a yellow circle (W A S D keys or arrows on a computer, a joystick on a phone).
2. **Listen.** A rising sitar line is "the sound of heat". Walk toward it until you find it. Stuck? Press **R** and Echo tells you the way in words ("the sound is straight ahead, about 40 metres away").
3. **Scan.** Press **Q** and Echo plays notes all around you, one direction at a time. The highest note points to the heat.
4. **Plant a tree.** Choose a mango, coconut palm or neem tree and plant it in the yellow circle.
5. **You are ready:** each year a heat wave is coming. Find the hot spot by ear, spend your coins on fixes, and protect more than half of Dhaka to win.

Along the way, small "Learn" cards explain: how NASA's Terra satellite (about 705 km up) measures how hot the ground is in squares about 1 km wide; what sonification means; why trees cool a street; and the urban heat island.

### Step 5 — How every year works

The city is split into **25 blocks**. The block names are invented (Rooftop Hill, Clay Pot Corner, Sari Street, Lotus Market…), but **each block's heat and greenness come from the matching part of a real NASA satellite image** of Dhaka.

1. **Echo's briefing.** Echo says which year it is and how hot the air got that April (from NASA's weather records).
2. **Find the heat.** Follow the sitar. Orange rings rise from the hot spot, and a yellow arrow helps if you get lost. When you arrive, Echo announces the block and its real ground temperature, for example *"Found a hot spot: Rooftop Hill, ground 32.3 °C."*
3. **Protect the blocks.** You get a small budget of coins. A mission card always says the one next thing to do.

    | Fix | Cost | What it does (game estimate) |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | Tree (mango, coconut palm or neem) | 1 coin | Shade and cooler air in its block |
    | White roof | 2 coins | Reflects sunlight instead of soaking it up |
    | Pond | 3 coins | Cools its block, and a little of the blocks next to it |
    | Cooling centre | 4 coins | Gives people shade, water and rest. It keeps people safe, but does **not** cool the city |

    Each time a block becomes safe, a big green banner says so: *"✔ Rooftop Hill is safe!"*

4. **The heat wave.** When the timer ends (or when you press **F**), the heat wave rolls in. Safe blocks stay calm and the music settles. Unsafe blocks turn red and the music stays out of tune.
5. **Echo's report.** You see:

    - how much of Dhaka you protected, and your stars (50 % = 1 star, 75 % = 2, 90 % = 3);
    - "What you learned this year";
    - one **quick question** (a right answer gives a bonus coin next year);
    - the **real NASA maps** of Dhaka for that April (ground heat and green plants), with a table of NASA numbers;
    - **a recovered piece of the message**.

    Then you go on to the next year, or play the year again.

### Step 6 — The six years

| Year | What is special | What you learn | Message piece recovered |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **2000** (NASA data) | One hot spot, plenty of time | Every place has a sound: hot is high. The ground is not the air. | *"We remember the old trees on our street…"* |
| **2010** (NASA data) | Two hot spots to find | One hot year next to another does not prove a trend: scientists need many years of data. | *"…the summers felt longer every year…"* |
| **2020** (NASA data) | **The heat is hidden.** No colours, no arrow: you must find it by ear. Then talk to Rina, the tea seller. | A satellite measures the ground surface, not how hot a person feels. Shade, wind and humidity matter too. | *"…someone listened to the ground, not only the sky…"* |
| **2024** (NASA data) | The hottest April air of the four years, and the fewest coins | Budgets force choices. A cooling centre protects people but does not cool the city. | *"…they kept people safe on the worst days…"* |
| **2035** (imagined) | First **you choose the future**: clean energy or coal power, parks and water or more concrete. | The choices people make now shape the heat of tomorrow. | *"…they chose what kind of city to build…"* |
| **2050** (imagined) | The final heat wave, with three hot spots, shaped by your choices and by everything you planted | White roofs reflect sunlight; rain cools the ground; the ending is ours to write. | *"…and now we can hear the birds again."* |

Two moments worth knowing about:

- **Rina the tea seller (2020)** explains that the satellite saw very hot ground that April, yet NASA's records show the April *air* was actually cooler than in 2010 or 2024. Ground heat and air heat are different things.
- If you protect less than half the city in 2050, Echo says: *"This ending is a warning, not a fate."* The game never ends on hopelessness.

### Step 7 — The Earth Record

- **You see and hear:** your six years played back as **one piece of music**, about one minute long, with a live visualiser. Each year is a 9.6-second section. The sitar plays the city's heat row by row from north to south, the tabla plays that April's rain, and the flute plays the green.
- **The clever part:** a year you protected ends on a calm "home" note. A year where the city was left in the heat ends on an unresolved, unfinished-sounding note. You can *hear* how well you did.
- **You get:** the full message, put back together, and a button to **download your record as a WAV sound file**.

### Step 8 — The ticket

A printable **Earth Listener Ticket** with your name, the average share of Dhaka you protected, your stars, the future you chose, your result for every year, every fact you learned, and your badges: **Heat Hunter** (found heat by ear alone), **City Cooler**, **Future Maker** and **Earth Learner** (quick questions right).

## 3. The NASA data in this game

### How the pictures become sound (simple version)

NASA publishes its heat map of Dhaka as a coloured image. Every colour stands for a temperature, and NASA provides the colour key. Our program reads each pixel's colour, uses NASA's own key to turn it back into a temperature, and averages it for each of the 25 blocks. That number becomes a note: hotter blocks play higher. The same is done for the green-plant map.

### NASA data used inside the game

| NASA data (plain name) | Official name | What it is, in plain words | Where you meet it in the game |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Ground heat maps of Dhaka** | MODIS land surface temperature (Terra satellite), 8-day daytime average, mid-April 2000, 2010, 2020 and 2024, from NASA GIBS / Worldview | How hot the ground, roofs and roads were, seen from space | Decides which blocks are hot spots and how hot each block is; drives the sitar; shown as maps in every report |
| **Green plant maps of Dhaka** | MODIS vegetation index (NDVI), April of the same years, from NASA GIBS / Worldview | How green each part of the city is (about 0.1 = bare or built-up, 0.6+ = lush) | Decides how many buildings and trees each block has; drives the flute; shown in every report |
| **Dhaka weather records** | NASA POWER, monthly air temperature, hottest day and rain for Dhaka, 2000–2024 | Air temperature and rain at one point in Dhaka | Echo's "hottest day" warnings; sets how strong each heat wave is; drives the tabla (rain); Rina's air-vs-ground lesson |
| **The world's temperature record** | NASA GISS GISTEMP v4, global yearly temperature change | How much warmer or cooler each year was worldwide, back to 1880 | The globe melody: one note per year |
| **Global temperature map animation** | NASA Scientific Visualization Studio, visualization 5060, "Global Temperature Anomalies from 1880 to 2022" | The coloured world map that turns from blue to red | The globe time-lapse at the start |
| **Picture of Earth** | NASA Blue Marble (Visible Earth) | A photo-like image of the whole Earth | The globe you fly down from |

| Challenge resource | How it connects to our game | Status |

## 4. Who the game is for, and the impact we hope for

### Who it is for

- **Children and teenagers**, and the parents and teachers who play with them. Each year takes about 3–4 minutes and ends with a short report and quiz, so it also works in a classroom.
- **Blind and low-vision players.** The game is built around sound. Every sound has a written caption, and the whole game can be played in *Text-only mode* with a screen reader.
- **People who find maps and charts hard**, who can hear the pattern instead of reading it.
- **People in Dhaka and Bangladesh**, who will recognise their own city: rickshaws, tea stalls, clay pots, saris drying on lines, mango and neem trees.
- **Anyone, anywhere**, with a browser. The game is small (about 4.5 MB) and needs no internet once it has loaded, which matters where connections are slow.

### The impact we hope for

- **Earth science you can hear.** NASA's satellite data becomes something you can feel and follow with your ears, not only a picture you have to see.
- **Open to more people.** Players who cannot see a heat map can still find the heat, protect the city and learn the science.
- **Honest science, explained simply.** Players learn real ideas that adults often mix up: the ground is not the air; one hot year is not a trend; the future is not fixed.
- **Solutions, not just fear.** Trees, water and light-coloured roofs really do cool cities. The game shows that money is limited and choices matter, and that protecting people is different from cooling a city.
- **A sense that "I can help."** Players become the *Guardian of Dhaka*. Even the hardest ending says *"a warning, not a fate."* The last piece of the message is hopeful: *"…and now we can hear the birds again."*

## Run locally

Install [Bun](https://bun.sh/), then run:

```bash
cd game
bun install --frozen-lockfile
bun run dev
```

Open `http://localhost:5173/`. Other commands, run from `game/`:

| Command | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| `bun run build` | Build the production site into `game/dist/` |
| `bun run preview` | Serve the production build locally |
| `bun run test` | Run the browser smoke test after building |
| `bun run check` | Build, run the smoke test and check game balance |

Browser tests require Chrome or a Playwright browser. Set `CHROME_PATH` if Chrome is not detected automatically.

## Deploy to Vercel

1. Push the repository to GitHub and import it as a new Vercel project.
2. Set **Root Directory** to **`game`** and choose the **Vite** framework preset.
3. Leave command overrides disabled. `game/vercel.json` configures:

   | Setting | Value |
   | --- | --- |
   | Install command | `bun install --frozen-lockfile` |
   | Build command | `bun run build` |
   | Output directory | `dist` |

4. Click **Deploy**. No environment variables, API keys, database or backend are needed.

NASA data, fonts and models are committed in `game/public/` and copied into the build. Asset-generation scripts do not need to run during deployment. The existing GitHub Pages workflow is separate from Vercel.

After deployment, check Start, the globe, the Dhaka tutorial, sound, models and text-only mode. Also check the phone layout.
